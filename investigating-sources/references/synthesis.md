# Synthesis

How to integrate across sources instead of summarizing them one at a time.
Summary lists what each source says; synthesis says what the sources *together*
establish, dispute, and leave open.

## Contents

- The move from summary to synthesis
- A working procedure

- Gap analysis

## The move from summary to synthesis

A summary is source-ordered: "Smith found A. Jones found B. Lee found C." A
synthesis is idea-ordered: "On question A, the evidence converges (Smith; Lee),
though one study dissents on grounds of X (Jones)." The unit of a synthesis is
the *claim or theme*, and sources are marshaled under it.

If a paragraph can be reordered by shuffling its sentences without loss, it is a
summary, not a synthesis.

## A working procedure

1. **Extract claims.** From each verified source, pull the findings relevant to
   the question. Keep them atomic — one assertion each — and tagged with the
   source key.
2. **Cluster by theme.** Group claims that speak to the same sub-question. The
   clusters become the sections of the synthesis.
3. **Within each cluster, map the landscape.** Where do sources agree? Where do
   they conflict? What is the quality of evidence on each side?
4. **State the cluster's bottom line** with its strength: strong convergence,
   mixed, contested, or thin. Cite the sources that carry it.
5. **Name what's missing** across clusters — the gaps.

## Handling agreement and conflict

**Agreement.** When multiple independent, quality sources reach the same
finding, say so and note that convergence strengthens the claim — but check they
are actually independent. Ten papers all citing one original study are one piece
of evidence wearing ten coats. Trace claims to their origin.

**Conflict.** When sources disagree, present both sides and compare the quality
of the evidence behind each (using `source_quality.md`). Do not silently drop
the side you find less congenial. Often the resolution is contextual: the
findings differ because the populations, methods, or time periods differ. Say
that when it is true. When two strong sources genuinely disagree and context
does not explain it, report it as an open question. "The evidence is mixed" is a
finding, not a failure.

## Gap analysis

Every synthesis should name what the literature does not yet answer:

- Questions no source addresses.
- Populations, contexts, or conditions that are understudied.
- Methodological weaknesses shared across the evidence base.
- Claims that rest on a single study and need replication.

Gaps are where the honest value of a review often lives; they tell the reader
what is genuinely unknown versus merely unmentioned.

## Anti-patterns

- **Cherry-picking.** Citing the supportive studies and omitting the
  contradicting ones. The disconfirming search in the workflow exists to prevent
  this; if you found contrary evidence, it appears in the synthesis.
- **False balance.** The opposite error: presenting a fringe or discredited
  position as equal to a well-supported consensus. Weight by evidence quality;
  do not manufacture a two-sided debate where the evidence is lopsided.
- **Citation laundering.** Treating "many papers say X" as strong evidence when
  those papers all trace to one weak original. Count independent evidence, not
  citations.
- **Vote counting.** Tallying how many studies found an effect versus not, as if
  each study counted equally regardless of size or quality. A single large
  rigorous study can outweigh several small flawed ones.
- **Synthesis by juxtaposition.** Placing sources side by side and letting the
  reader do the integrating. Do the integrating yourself: state the relationship
  between the findings.
- **Overreach.** Drawing a conclusion the pooled evidence does not support.
  Scope the claim to what the sources actually establish.
