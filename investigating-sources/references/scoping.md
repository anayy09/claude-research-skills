# Scoping

How to turn a request into a precise, answerable question. Used in the SCOPE
step of every mode, and as the whole of `scope` mode when the user has no
question yet.

## Contents

- What a good question looks like
- The scoping checklist
- Running scope mode (guided)
- When a question cannot converge

## What a good question looks like

A researchable question is specific about what, who, and under what conditions,
and it admits an answer that evidence could confirm or disconfirm. Compare:

- Vague: "AI in education."
- Researchable: "What is the evidence that AI tutoring systems improve maths
  attainment for secondary students, compared with traditional instruction?"

The second names the intervention (AI tutoring), the population (secondary
students), the outcome (maths attainment), and a comparison (traditional
instruction). It can be answered with sources; the first cannot.

A useful informal test: five dimensions worth pinning down are whether the
question is feasible to answer with available evidence, genuinely interesting or
useful, adds something beyond restating common knowledge, ethical to pursue, and
relevant to the user's actual need. Do not turn this into a scoring ritual; use
it as a checklist for what might be underspecified.

## The scoping checklist

Before searching, write down:

- **The question**, as one clear sentence.
- **Sub-questions**, two or three, that the main question decomposes into.
- **In scope / out of scope** — what the deliverable will and will not cover.
  Explicit exclusions prevent scope creep and set honest expectations.
- **Success criteria** — what would make this a good answer. What must be
  covered? What kind of evidence would settle it?

This frame is what CHALLENGE and AUDIT later check the work against, so it is
worth getting right.

## Running scope mode (guided)

When the user has only a vague interest, help them find the question through
genuine questions — not by proposing an answer and asking them to agree.

Good scoping questions surface:

- **The real interest.** "What drew you to this? Is there a specific case,
  claim, or decision behind it?"
- **The constraints.** "Is this for a paper, a decision, curiosity? How deep do
  you need to go?"
- **The boundaries.** "Are you interested in a particular population, place, or
  time period? Anything you specifically want to exclude?"
- **What would count as an answer.** "When you're done, what would you want to be
  able to say or decide?"

Ask one or two at a time, not a barrage. Reflect their answers back as a
sharpening question. The output is a refined research question plus a
recommendation of which mode to run next. Do not steer them toward a conclusion
you have already formed; the point is to find *their* question.

Never disguise an answer as a question ("Wouldn't you agree that X?"). Ask things
you do not already know the answer to.

## When a question cannot converge

If, after a few rounds, the question is still too broad to search, offer a
concrete way forward rather than looping:

- Propose two or three candidate questions at different scopes and let the user
  pick.
- Suggest starting with a `lit-review` to map the territory, which often reveals
  the sharper question.
- Narrow to the single most tractable sub-question and note the rest as future
  directions.

Converging on a good question is progress even when no report has been written
yet.
