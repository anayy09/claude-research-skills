# Reasoning Checks

The CHALLENGE step stress-tests the emerging conclusion before it is written.
Run the draft argument against these checks and, where one bites, return to
SEARCH rather than writing around the hole.

## How to run the challenge

State, in one sentence, the strongest objection a skeptical domain expert would
raise to your conclusion. Then check the argument against the categories below.
This is not a box-ticking exercise; the point is to genuinely try to break your
own conclusion. If it survives, the deliverable is stronger and you can say so.
If it does not, you found a problem before the reader did.

## Evidence and inference errors

- **Cherry-picking** — building the case only from supportive evidence. Confirm
  the disconfirming search actually happened.
- **Anecdote as evidence** — a vivid single case standing in for a pattern. One
  example is an illustration, not proof.
- **Correlation treated as causation** — see causal errors below.
- **Overgeneralization** — extending a finding beyond the population, setting, or
  conditions it was established in.
- **Appeal to authority over evidence** — "an expert said so" substituting for
  what the evidence shows. Authority indicates where to look, not what is true.
- **Ecological fallacy** — inferring about individuals from group-level data (or
  the reverse).
- **Survivorship bias** — reasoning only from the cases that made it into view
  (successful companies, published studies) and ignoring those that did not.
- **Publication bias** — the literature over-represents positive findings; null
  results are underpublished. A pile of significant results may partly reflect
  what got printed, not what is true.

## Causal errors

- **Post hoc** — B followed A, therefore A caused B. Sequence is not causation.
- **Confounding** — an unmeasured third factor drives both the supposed cause and
  effect. The classic reason correlation is not causation.
- **Reverse causation** — the arrow may point the other way (does stress cause
  poor sleep, or poor sleep cause stress?).
- **Bidirectional / feedback** — cause and effect reinforce each other; a
  one-directional story oversimplifies.

When a source claims causation, check whether its design can actually support a
causal claim (see the evidence hierarchy in `source_quality.md`). Observational
data rarely can on its own.

## Selection and framing errors

- **Selection bias** — the sample is not representative of the population the
  claim is about.
- **False dilemma** — presenting two options as exhaustive when others exist.
- **Framing effects** — the same data described to steer the reader ("90%
  survived" vs "10% died"). Report neutrally.
- **Base-rate neglect** — ignoring how common something is at baseline when
  judging a result's significance.
- **Nirvana fallacy** — rejecting a real option because it is not perfect, or an
  imperfect study because it is not ideal.

## Formal fallacies

- **Affirming the consequent** — "If P then Q; Q; therefore P." Q can have other
  causes.
- **Denying the antecedent** — "If P then Q; not P; therefore not Q." Q can
  arise another way.
- **Undistributed middle** — "All A are B; C is B; therefore C is A." Sharing a
  property does not establish identity.
- **Equivocation** — a key term shifts meaning across the argument.

## The two questions to always ask

1. **"What would change my mind?"** If nothing could, the conclusion is not
   empirical — it is a commitment. Name the evidence that would overturn it and
   confirm you looked for it.
2. **"So what, and how sure?"** State why the conclusion matters and how
   confident the evidence warrants being. Overconfidence is as much a failure as
   error; calibrate the strength of the claim to the strength of the evidence.
