# Journal and article-type conventions

Derived from publisher author guides and from the structure of published
articles in the venues named. Figures such as word limits are given as
typical values checked against the guides in September 2026; they change,
and the author instructions for the specific journal and article type
override anything here. The point of this file is the shape of the
expectations, and the split between what is general and what is local.

## 1. Principles that hold across Q1 venues

These are not house rules; they follow from how editors and reviewers read.

- **The abstract is a self-contained account** with the headline result and
  number, the comparator, and the scope. It is consistent with the body to
  the digit. Structured or not depends on the venue (section 3).
- **The Introduction ends on the specific contribution**, stated as what was
  done and found, not as aims. Broad importance gets one or two sentences.
- **Methods are sufficient for reproduction** and give the reason for every
  non-obvious choice in one sentence. Methods report procedures, not
  results, and do not argue.
- **Results report; Discussion interprets.** Nature-family Results sections
  carry interpretive subheadings and light interpretation inline; IEEE and
  medical journals keep Results drier. Either way, the Discussion is not a
  second Results section.
- **Limitations are stated once, plainly, with mechanism**, in the
  Discussion or a Limitations subsection. Every Q1 venue expects them;
  their absence is read as naivety.
- **Claims are proportional to evidence** and stated at the same strength
  everywhere they appear.
- **One home per fact.** Redundancy between sections is a signal of
  patching. The abstract is the only place that restates by design.
- **No reviewer-facing text.** The clean manuscript stands on its own; the
  response letter carries the account of change (Springer's revision
  guidance is explicit that the letter describes the revisions and the
  manuscript shows them via track changes or highlighting).
- **No self-promotion vocabulary.** "Novel", "robust", "significant" (in
  the non-statistical sense), "state-of-the-art" as an adjective, and
  "groundbreaking" are cut across all these venues; editors at Nature-family
  and Cell titles remove them in production if the authors do not.
- **Display items are cited in order and every item earns its place.**
  Venues with display-item caps (Nature 6, Science 4 to 5, Nature Portfolio
  8) enforce this; those without caps still expect it.
- **Data, code, and materials availability statements** are required or
  strongly expected at essentially every Q1 venue in the sciences and
  engineering. Absent statements are a desk-return trigger at Nature
  Portfolio, Cell Press, PLOS, and most medical journals.
- **Competing interests, funding, author contributions (CRediT at Elsevier
  and many others), and ethics or IRB statements** are structural sections
  and carry no argument.

## 2. Section-by-section content rules

| Section | Belongs | Does not belong | Common revision damage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Abstract | Problem in one clause, approach, headline result with number and comparator, scope, implication | Citations (most venues), undefined acronyms, methods detail, hedged non-results | Numbers out of sync with body after re-analysis; reviewer-prompted qualifiers appended |
| Introduction | Specific problem, what prior work established and left open, contribution stated as findings | Extended textbook background, results, a second related-work section | Growth from pre-empting objections; contributions list padded |
| Related work / Background | Prior approaches by idea, what each showed, where it fell short; the gap emerging from the critique | Uncritical lists, chronology for its own sake, the authors' own results | Reviewer-named papers bolted on without integration |
| Methods | Data, procedure, analysis, and one-sentence reasons for non-obvious choices; enough to reproduce | Results, interpretation, defense of validity, restated motivation | "We chose X, which is standard and appropriate" justifications; sensitivity analyses narrated as additions |
| Results | Findings with numbers, uncertainty, and comparator; figures and tables cited in order; enough interpretation to follow | Restated methods, extended discussion, background, claims beyond the data | Subgroup analyses added where the reviewer asked rather than where the logic goes; hedges on every sentence |
| Discussion | Meaning of the main result, position against prior work, mechanism where inferable, limitations with mechanism, implications | Restated background, restated results with the same numbers, new results, a summary of the paper | Defensive paragraphs; limitations scattered; restated Introduction on top |
| Conclusion (if separate) | What was shown, the condition under which it holds, what it changes; shorter than the Discussion | New claims, new numbers, a compressed Discussion, an extended future-work list | Grows every round because a reviewer said it ended abruptly |
| Figure and table captions | Standalone description: what is shown, how, what the marks mean, n, error definition | Interpretation, narrative, revision notes | Caption describes the old panel layout |
| Supplement | Reproduction detail, extra analyses, extended tables; each item pointed to from the main text | Content the argument depends on and that reviewers must read to assess it | Reviewer-requested analyses hidden in the supplement while the main text claims them |

## 3. Venue families

### Nature (flagship)

Source: Nature's formatting guide (nature.com/nature/authors/gta), checked
September 2026.

- Articles open with a fully referenced **summary paragraph** of about 200
  words, separate from the main text, aimed at readers outside the
  discipline: two or three sentences introducing the field, the background
  and rationale, the main conclusion introduced by "Here we show" or
  equivalent, and two or three sentences on general context. No numbers,
  abbreviations, or measurements unless essential.
- Typical length: about 2,500 words including the summary paragraph for a
  six-page Article with four modest display items, about 4,300 for an
  eight-page Article with five or six. A "modest" display item with legend is
  about a quarter page.
- Main text has no Introduction heading; Results subheadings; Discussion;
  then **Methods after the references**, written to allow reproduction, with
  its own length guidance. Extended Data figures and tables carry the
  overflow and are peer reviewed.
- Numbered superscript references, kept to a few dozen.
- Editorial reading is cross-disciplinary: the first paragraph decides.

### Nature Portfolio research journals (Nature Medicine, Nature Methods, Nature Biotechnology, Nature Machine Intelligence, and others)

- Same architecture: unstructured abstract of about 150 words, main text
  of about 3,000 words, Methods after references with no fixed cap but
  expected to be concise, Extended Data, typically up to 8 display items,
  numbered superscript references.
- Reporting Summary (life sciences, or the equivalent) and reporting
  checklists are mandatory and cross-checked against Methods.
- Nature Methods and Nature Biotechnology weight reproducibility and code
  availability heavily; a Methods section a reader could not follow gets
  sent back.

### Nature Communications, npj journals, Scientific Reports

- Structured as Introduction, Results, Discussion, Methods, in that order,
  with Methods inside the main text. Abstract about 150 words. Main text
  about 5,000 words including Methods at Nature Communications; npj titles
  and Scientific Reports are more flexible. Display items up to about 10.
- Same reference style and reporting requirements as the rest of the
  portfolio. The extra length is for Methods and Results, not for
  Discussion.

### Science

- Abstract of about 125 words, one paragraph. A one-sentence summary is
  supplied separately.
- Reports and Research Articles are both short in the main text (order of
  2,500 to 4,500 words including some methods), with Materials and Methods
  largely in the supplementary materials. Display-item caps of roughly 4 to
  5. Numbered references in parentheses, capped by article type.
- Main text can run without headings in shorter formats; Research Articles
  use Introduction, Results, Discussion.

### Cell Press (Cell, Cell Systems, Cell Reports Medicine, Patterns, and others)

Source: Cell Press STAR Methods and Key Resources Table guidance (cell.com
and elsevier.com), checked September 2026.

- **Summary** of up to about 150 words, **Highlights** (three to four
  bullets, at most about 85 characters each), an **eTOC blurb** of a few
  sentences, and a **graphical abstract**. These are all part of the
  manuscript and are edited with it.
- **STAR Methods**: a structured methods section that is part of the main
  article, not supplement, with a **Key Resources Table** listing every
  reagent, dataset, software, and model used, plus Resource Availability,
  Experimental Model and Subject Details, Method Details, and Quantification
  and Statistical Analysis subsections.
- Longer Introductions than Nature or Science, expected to be mechanistic
  and comprehensive. Results subheadings are declarative findings.
- Reference style is author-year in text, numbered in the list.

### Medical journals (The Lancet family, NEJM, JAMA Network, BMJ, Annals)

- **Structured abstracts** are the rule, with venue-specific headings:
  Lancet uses Background, Methods, Findings, Interpretation, Funding; JAMA
  uses Importance, Objective, Design/Setting/Participants,
  Exposures or Interventions, Main Outcomes and Measures, Results,
  Conclusions and Relevance; BMJ uses Objectives, Design, Setting,
  Participants, Interventions, Main outcome measures, Results, Conclusions.
  Word limits are longer than Nature's, in the 250 to 350 range.
- Lancet journals require a **Research in context** panel (Evidence before
  this study, Added value of this study, Implications of all the available
  evidence) that describes the literature search performed. JAMA requires
  **Key Points** (Question, Findings, Meaning). These are manuscript
  elements and must agree with the abstract.
- IMRaD is strict. Discussion opens with the principal findings, then
  strengths and limitations, then comparison with other studies, then
  implications; BMJ prescribes this order.
- **Reporting guidelines are mandatory** and the completed checklist is
  submitted (section 5). Trial and observational study registration is
  stated in Methods. Effect sizes with confidence intervals are expected
  over bare p-values.
- Author contributions, data-sharing statements, and declarations follow
  ICMJE.

### Elsevier engineering, computing, and medical informatics journals (Medical Image Analysis, Artificial Intelligence in Medicine, Journal of Biomedical Informatics, Computer Methods and Programs in Biomedicine, Pattern Recognition, and many others)

- **Highlights**: three to five bullets of at most 85 characters each,
  submitted separately, stating findings not topics. Graphical abstract
  often optional.
- Abstract usually unstructured, 150 to 250 words; some titles (JBI, CMPB)
  accept or require structured abstracts.
- Numbered sections (1 Introduction, 2 Related work or Background, 3
  Methods or Materials and methods, 4 Results, 5 Discussion, 6 Conclusion)
  are the norm. Contribution list in the Introduction is accepted.
- **CRediT author statement**, Declaration of competing interests, and Data
  availability are required structural sections.
- Length limits vary by title; many have none but editors push back on
  length in review.

### IEEE Transactions and Journals

- Abstract 150 to 250 words, single paragraph, no citations, no undefined
  abbreviations. **Index Terms** follow the abstract.
- Roman-numeral sections (I. INTRODUCTION), numbered equations, a
  Conclusion section is expected. A bulleted contribution list at the end
  of the Introduction is standard.
- Page-based limits with overlength charges rather than word limits.
- Related Work as its own section is common; a Discussion section is often
  folded into Results or omitted in favor of a longer Conclusion, which
  makes the "Conclusion carries no new claims" rule slightly softer here.

### ACM journals and conferences

- CCS concepts and keywords. Structure close to IEEE. Reproducibility
  badges (artifacts available, evaluated) are a submission-time decision
  and shape the artifact appendix.

### Machine learning conferences (NeurIPS, ICML, ICLR) and their journals (TMLR, JMLR)

- Page limits on the main body (on the order of 8 to 10 pages), unlimited
  appendix. Contribution bullets in the Introduction are standard.
- NeurIPS requires a **Limitations** discussion and a paper checklist;
  broader-impact statements are expected where relevant. Reviewers check
  the checklist against the text.
- The review-time **rebuttal** is a short reply within the review system,
  not a revised manuscript; changes are promised there and made in the
  camera-ready. The boundary rule still applies: the camera-ready carries no
  rebuttal language.
- TMLR and JMLR have no page limits and expect fuller Methods and
  Discussion than the conference versions.

### Springer journals, BMC series, PLOS

- BMC and many Springer titles require a **structured abstract**
  (Background, Methods, Results, Conclusions) and a **Declarations** block
  (ethics, consent, availability, competing interests, funding, author
  contributions).
- PLOS ONE has no length limit and judges technical soundness rather than
  perceived importance; PLOS Medicine and PLOS Computational Biology use
  structured abstracts and author summaries.
- No length limit does not mean no length expectation: reviewers at these
  venues still flag redundancy.

## 4. Article-type modifiers

Within a venue, the article type changes the shape more than the venue does.

- **Letter, Brief Communication, Short Report.** No section headings or
  minimal ones; the Introduction is one paragraph; Methods are compressed or
  in the supplement; one to three display items. Every redundancy rule is
  stricter.
- **Resource, Dataset, Tools and Resources.** Methods and validation
  dominate; the "contribution" is the artifact and its documented
  properties; Discussion is short and about use, not mechanism.
- **Analysis (Nature Portfolio).** A study of existing data or methods;
  structure like an Article, with the analysis design in Methods and
  extensive Extended Data.
- **Review, Perspective.** No Methods except a search strategy (for
  systematic reviews, PRISMA applies). Structure by theme. The redundancy
  rules apply to the argument, not to a results narrative.
- **Registered Report.** Stage 1 is Introduction plus Methods with
  proposed analyses; Stage 2 adds Results and Discussion and declares any
  deviation from the approved protocol in the manuscript.
- **Case report, technical note, comment.** Venue-specific templates; follow
  them exactly.

### Pre-registered studies, registered reports, and audits

Papers whose contribution includes having registered targets in advance
have a specific failure mode: the registration becomes the subject of the
prose. Convention across venues that publish them (Nature Human Behaviour,
Cortex and other Registered Reports outlets, BMJ, PLOS, most
Nature Portfolio and Springer titles with a pre-registration policy):

- One Methods subsection states what was registered, where (repository or
  registry, with identifier and date), and what changed since. That is the
  only place the word "registered" carries an argument.
- Results reports registered and exploratory analyses in the same voice,
  with the distinction carried by a table column or a one-word label
  ("registered", "exploratory"), not by repeated prose assertions.
- Failed registered predictions are reported as results: target, measured
  value, interval, verdict, in one table. Interpretation of the failures is
  one Discussion paragraph. Their status as "reported because registered" is
  not stated; the table is the statement.
- Provenance and reproducibility machinery (run ledgers, hash checks,
  automated reference verification) is one Methods paragraph or a
  supplement. It is not repeated in figure captions or in the contribution
  list.

### Where a literature search belongs in a research article

When a research article includes a structured literature search (to
establish that a baseline is absent, to position a claim), the search is a
method: protocol, sources, dates, screening counts go in Methods (one
paragraph) or a supplement with a PRISMA-style flow if the venue's article
type expects it. The Introduction or Background cites what the search found
and states the gap; it does not describe how the search was run. A table of
every screened study with its bearing on the paper is supplementary.
Rapid-review and scoping-review limits are stated once, in Limitations.

## 5. Reporting guidelines by study type

These determine what Methods and Results must contain. The checklist is
submitted with the manuscript at medical and many life-science venues and
is increasingly requested elsewhere.

| Study type | Guideline |
|---|---|
| Randomized trial | CONSORT (2025 update) |
| Observational (cohort, case-control, cross-sectional) | STROBE |
| Diagnostic accuracy | STARD |
| Prediction model, including ML | TRIPOD+AI (2024); TRIPOD-Cluster for clustered data |
| AI intervention trial protocol / report | SPIRIT-AI, CONSORT-AI; DECIDE-AI for early clinical evaluation |
| Systematic review / meta-analysis | PRISMA 2020; PRISMA-S for searches |
| Animal studies | ARRIVE 2.0 |
| Qualitative research | COREQ, SRQR |
| Economic evaluation | CHEERS 2022 |
| Quality improvement | SQUIRE 2.0 |

The EQUATOR Network catalog is the source for anything not listed. When a
reviewer asks for a missing item that a guideline requires, it goes into the
manuscript in the guideline's expected location, not into the response
letter alone.

## 6. Deriving conventions for an unfamiliar venue

When the target is not covered above, or when a listed venue may have
changed its rules:

1. Read the current author instructions for the specific article type.
   Note: abstract format and limit, main-text limit and what it includes,
   display-item cap, Methods placement, required elements (highlights, key
   points, graphical abstract, statements), reference style and cap,
   reporting guideline requirements, supplementary policy.
2. Open two or three recent published articles of the same type in that
   journal. Record the section headings actually used, the length of the
   Introduction relative to the whole, whether Results subheadings are
   declarative, where limitations sit, how the Conclusion is handled, and
   how figures are cited.
3. Where the instructions and the published articles disagree, the
   published articles show what the editors accept; the instructions show
   what they will return for. Satisfy the instructions; match the articles.
4. Record the derived conventions in the revision ledger under venue notes
   so later rounds do not re-derive them.

The general principles in section 1 apply regardless. What varies is the
container shape, never the requirement that the manuscript stand on its own
without the review process visible in it.
